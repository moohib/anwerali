Reflections

I have maintained a habit of reflecting on my life, learning, and design practice. Over the years, these reflections have helped me make sense of the people, experiences, challenges, and opportunities that shape my journey. As personal, academic, and professional responsibilities continue to grow, writing has become a way of slowing down, thinking critically, and finding meaning in everyday experiences. By sharing these reflections, I hope to contribute to ongoing conversations, spark new ideas, and perhaps help others reflect on their own journeys ↓

Cultivating Responsible Design in Pakistan

One of the questions that I often ask myself is: how do we teach design in a world in crisis? For those of us navigating the studio spaces of Pakistani higher education institutions, this is not merely an academic question but an urgent, daily confrontation. Across rural, urban, and semi-urban learning environments in Pakistan, students constantly navigate the tension between globally dominant design theories and local socio-cultural realities. Considering the current deepening crises in the country, it seems less of a primary concern. Still, as a designer and educator, I think one cannot ignore this fundamental question that directly links to our own existence.

While graphic design has historically been closely tied to commerce and systems of power, contemporary design practice increasingly engages with social, environmental, and humanitarian concerns. A truly responsible designer has a humane responsibility to empathize with fellow human beings. Design is a powerful, problem-solving activity, and in a country facing major economic and educational challenges, the opportunities for design-based intervention are endless. To bridge this gap, we must turn to responsive pedagogy: a teaching practice that is responsive, responsible, and grounded in active reflection.

To understand responsible design, we must first look inward. As educators and practitioners, we often find ourselves caught in a routine. This habit of doing things can easily kill the creative spirit and obscure our core objectives. To combat this stagnation, I often employ the Onion Metaphor (not Hannah Arendt’s one). It also helps students to learn their position.

I give an example of an onion to my students, suggesting that we operate within multiple interconnected layers: The Self: our physical, psychological, and historical core. The Studio/Classroom: the immediate environment where learning and experimentation occur. The Institution: administrative structures, policies, and power dynamics. Society: the broader cultural, political, and economic context in which design functions.

By peeling back these layers, we move beyond simple reporting and enter the realm of reflexivity. While reflection involves looking back on an event, reflexivity is action-oriented. It demands that we critically examine our own positions and biases, acknowledging how our socio-economic and cultural backgrounds shape our teaching and design practices. This self-exploration is demanding and requires stepping out of our comfort zones, but it is the only way to pave the way toward responsive, transformative teaching. One can explore Donald Schön's reflective practice model and Stephen Brookfield's proposed lenses for further investigation.

These ideas are only meaningful if they can shape what happens in the classroom. Over the past several semesters, I have sought to embed real-world social concerns directly into communication design projects, replacing purely commercial briefs with opportunities for civic engagement and critical reflection.

Three projects from my teaching practice stand out as particularly meaningful examples of responsive and responsible design education. When devastating floods displaced over 11 million people in Pakistan, leaving sixty percent of the country underwater. Following the devastating floods in Pakistan, I introduced an Urdu Expressive Typography project that invited students to respond creatively to the crisis through language and form.

To push students to focus on raw, conceptual thinking, we implemented two strict constraints: students had to design in Urdu script, which significantly reduced the risk of online plagiarism and connected them directly to local discourse. For the visual constraint, the deliverables had to be flat, black-and-white designs. By stripping away the seductive distraction of color, students were encouraged to think deeply about limited typographic possibilities, legibility, and structural meaning.

The results were transformative. Students produced highly expressive visual interpretations that revealed both technical skill and a deep engagement with the social realities surrounding them. For a deeper discussion of the project, the full case study can be accessed here Urdu Expressive Lettering.

In another studio iteration, we challenged students to address current global humanitarian crises through the lens of Deconstructionist Typography. Deconstructionism in graphic design challenges conventional typographic rules by treating letterforms as fluid, unstable, and open to reinterpretation.

Students designed typographic messages (combining English and Urdu in bi-scriptural layouts) to register their voices against international crises. By utilizing deconstructionist techniques, they broke fixed associations of meaning and context, driving the audience to engage with the message actively. This project demonstrated that typography is more than an aesthetic exercise. It can serve as a powerful vehicle for public expression, critique, and social commentary.

The third case involves an Integrated Design Learning project. Students conducted a field visit to the Horizon: House of Light School (HOLS) in Pind Begwal, a free school serving underprivileged children. HOLS provides free education, meals, and uniforms, but faced severe budget constraints due to the country's economic downturn.

This visit served as a profound "critical incident" that awakened an ethical impulse in our students. Seeing children who motivated themselves to study despite extreme adversity made our students realize their duty as ethical designers. Returning to the university studio, they worked on concrete design interventions. This project integrated real-life problems into design learning, demonstrating that the best design solution is one that is practical, cost-effective, and directly serves the community.

Integrating social action into the design studio is not without its challenges. Students often experience a sense of fatigue or boredom when transitioning from commercial work to rigorous, community-centered design. As educators, we must also battle institutional space constraints, administrative delays, and lack of human and material resources.

Yet, the reward of responsive pedagogy is the creation of a democratic and autonomous practice. By inviting feedback, encouraging students to write reflective journals, and engaging with real-world communities, we break the capitalistic monopoly over visual communication. Responsible design is not a static set of rules; it is an active, evolving dialogue. It demands that we step out of our comfort zones, look through unfamiliar angles, and use our visual skills to build brave, socially conscious spaces.

Learning from the TEVTA Project

One fine day in March, I decided to take a small pedagogical risk. For several years, I had been questioning whether design education could move beyond simulated classroom briefs and engage with the complexities of the real world. While hypothetical projects are useful for developing technical skills, I often wondered whether they truly prepare students to navigate uncertainty, communicate with stakeholders, and frame problems situated within social and institutional realities. This question led my students and me to the Technical Education and Vocational Training Authority (TEVTA) in Gujrat, Pakistan.

Our visit began as a simple field trip, but for me it represented something much larger. We met the leadership team, discussed TEVTA's objectives, and explored the possibilities for collaboration. During our conversations, it became clear that the institution was managing numerous initiatives and facing a range of communication and design challenges. As a design educator, I immediately saw opportunities for students to work with authentic constraints rather than artificially constructed assignments.

Walking through the wood workshop was particularly revealing. Students observed the entire production process, from raw timber storage to cutting, sanding, assembly, and finishing. They photographed machinery, documented workflows, and spoke with technical staff. What might have appeared to be an ordinary vocational facility suddenly became a site of inquiry. Students were not simply gathering visual references; they were learning how to observe systems, understand users, and recognize the relationships between people, processes, and environments.

Yet the most significant learning was happening within me.

I remember questioning whether I should even proceed with the project. Would students be willing to work on a real client brief? Would they embrace the uncertainty and responsibility that comes with external collaboration? Was I prepared to take on the additional workload and unpredictability that such projects demand? At the time, these concerns felt very real. Few people around me were integrating industry-facing projects into studio teaching, and leaving the comfort of conventional assignments felt risky.

A few days later, I brought TEVTA's publications into the classroom and posed a direct question to my students: Are you willing to leave your comfort zone? I made it clear that participation required commitment, curiosity, and resilience. To my surprise, the response was overwhelmingly positive. Their enthusiasm reassured me, although I still wondered whether they fully understood the challenges ahead.

What followed was one of the most rewarding studio experiences I had witnessed. Students began discussing ideas, identifying opportunities, and framing design problems from multiple perspectives. Rather than asking what looked visually appealing, they started asking what would be useful, meaningful, and relevant. Their conversations shifted from aesthetics alone to purpose, audience, communication, and impact.

The experience reinforced an important lesson for me: design education is not merely about teaching students how to make things. It is about helping them understand why things need to be made, for whom, and under what circumstances. Real-world projects expose students to ambiguity, negotiation, collaboration, and responsibility, all of which are essential dimensions of professional practice.

Looking back, the project marked an important moment in my teaching journey. It demonstrated that when students are trusted with authentic challenges, they often exceed our expectations. More importantly, it reminded me that meaningful design pedagogy begins when educators are willing to step beyond familiar routines and create opportunities for students to engage with the world as active contributors rather than passive learners.
